How a machine finds the right page: when the material is too big to send to the model, how do you find the right few pieces to send instead?

PipelineEmbeddingsChunkingVector databasesRerankingHybrid search
    01

    You already built the crudest possible version

    Your summariser fetches a page, takes the whole thing, and pastes it into the prompt. Every word. Then it asks a question about it.

    That is retrieval. Specifically, it is retrieval where you retrieve everything and hope the answer is in there. It works for one web page. It falls apart the moment the material is bigger than the prompt, and almost all real material is: a company handbook, a year of support tickets, a textbook.

    Visualizer · What the crude version throws awayMAX_CHARS = 12,000

    Your scraper truncates, and throws away most of the document without knowing whether the answer was in the part it cut. That is the gap this class fills. The question for today is narrow and it is the whole of RAG:

    When the material is too big to send, how do you find the right few pieces to send instead?
    02

    Why RAG exists

    RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. Before the model answers, you retrieve the most relevant pieces of your own material and put them in the prompt. The model answers from those. No retraining. No fine-tuning. It is an open-book exam instead of a closed-book one.

    Compare · Closed book vs open bookToggle

    The four problems it solves

    ProblemWhat it looks likeWhat RAG does
    Knowledge cutoffIt does not know what happened after training endedFetch today's document
    Private dataIt has never seen your HR policy, your contracts, your ticketsSearch your own material
    InventionWhen unsure, it produces something plausibleGround the answer in real text
    No sourcesAsk "where did you get that" and there is nothingReturn the passage it used

    The first two are capability problems. The second two are the trust problems from Class 3, and RAG is the main structural answer to them.

    Important

    The fourth one is underrated. In Class 3 you learned that a confident answer tells you nothing about whether it is true. A retrieved answer can show you the passage, which means somebody can check it in five seconds. That changes what you can responsibly build.

    Why not just fine-tune a model on our data instead? Common doubt

    Three reasons it is usually the wrong first move. It is expensive and slow to redo every time your data changes, and your data changes constantly. It still cannot cite a source. And it does not stop invention. RAG is faster, cheaper, traceable, and almost always what you want first.

    03

    The pipeline, and the two phases people confuse

    Strip the jargon and the whole thing is a librarian sitting between the question and the model. You ask a friend about an 800-page book they have never read, and they guess. Instead, you find the three most relevant pages, hand them over, and then ask. Now they answer from real text. The librarian who finds those three pages is the only new thing in this class.

    If indexing is slow, does my app get slower as I add documents? Common doubt

    Indexing does, and your users never see it. Querying barely changes, because the search structures are built to scale: going from ten thousand chunks to ten million adds a handful of steps, not a million. Section 6 says why.

    04

    Embeddings: why meaning needs coordinates

    Before you can search by meaning, you need meaning in a form a computer can compare. That is an embedding, and once you see what it does the rest of RAG stops being mysterious.

    Why keyword search is not enough

    Someone searches your shoe catalogue for "running shoes". Your best product is titled "marathon footwear". Keyword search returns nothing, because not one word matches. You need a way to find things that mean the same, not things that spell the same.

    The idea: meaning as a position

    Put people on a chart with height on one axis and weight on the other. Each person becomes a point. People of similar build end up near each other, and you can ask "who is most similar to this person" by measuring distance. You turned a description into coordinates and got comparison for free.

    An embedding does exactly that for text, except with hundreds or thousands of axes instead of two, and the axes are not things you can name. Similar meanings land near each other. Different meanings land far apart.

    Important

    This is the single idea the whole class rests on. "Find the relevant chunk" becomes "find the nearest point", and finding nearby points is something computers are extremely good at.

    The part that convinces people

    Take the vector for king, subtract man, add woman, and you land very close to queen. That is arithmetic, on meaning. The model was never taught what royalty or gender are; those relationships fell out of learning to predict text, which is Class 1's self-supervision lesson showing up again in a different form.

    How similarity is actually measured

    Cosine similarity is the angle between two arrows from the origin. Pointing the same way scores 1. At right angles, 0. Opposite, -1. Why the angle and not the straight-line distance: a long document and a short sentence about the same topic produce arrows of different lengths but similar direction. Angle captures "about the same thing" while ignoring "says more about it".

    Visualizer · Angle vs distanceReal arithmetic
    1.00cosine similarity
    1.0straight-line distance

    Set the angle to 0° and change the length: the cosine stays at 1 while the straight-line distance changes. Same topic, different amount said about it.

    Choosing an embedding model

    You do not need to build one, and you do need to pick one.

    DecisionWhat to think about
    General or domain-specificA general model is fine for most things. Legal, medical and code have specialised models that do noticeably better.
    English or multilingualA multilingual model lets a Hindi question find an English passage. An English-only model will not.
    Size and dimensionsBigger vectors capture more nuance, cost more to store and compare. More is not automatically better.
    Hosted or localHosted is one API call. Local keeps your text on your own machines, which matters for regulated data.
    Try it · The one rule that catches everybodySame model for documents and questions
    Important

    Two models produce two different coordinate systems, and distances between them are meaningless. Change your embedding model and you must re-index everything.

    Is an embedding the same as a token from Class 1? Optional doubt

    No, and they are easy to confuse. A token is a chunk of text the model reads, with no meaning attached. An embedding is a position in meaning-space for a whole piece of text. Tokens are about reading, embeddings are about comparing.

    05

    Chunking: how you cut decides what you can find

    You cannot embed a 300-page book as one vector. The meaning of the whole book averages out into something that is close to nothing. So you cut it into pieces first, and how you cut matters a great deal. A chunk is the unit of retrieval: whatever you cut is what gets found and handed to the model.

    Four strategies worth knowing

    StrategyHow it cutsWhere it fits
    Fixed sizeEvery N characters, regardless of contentSimplest. Cuts mid-sentence and mid-word. Fine for uniform text, bad for most things.
    RecursiveTries paragraph breaks first, then sentences, then wordsThe sane default. Respects natural boundaries while keeping sizes roughly even.
    SemanticCuts where the topic changesBest quality, most expensive, because it needs a model to judge the boundaries.
    Structure-awareUses the document's own headings and sectionsFree quality when the document has structure: markdown, HTML, a contract with clauses.
    Visualizer · ChunkingSame passage, different cuts

    Question: "How long do I have to report a damaged item?" The answer sentence is outlined when a chunk holds it whole.

    0chunks
    0cut mid-sentence

    The passage is a made-up handbook. Semantic cuts are hand-marked at topic changes, since really judging them needs a model. Size and overlap do not apply to semantic or structure-aware cuts.

    The trade-off you will actually tune

    Big chunks

    High recall, low precision. The answer is probably in there, surrounded by a lot of irrelevant text that dilutes it.

    Small chunks

    High precision, low recall. When you find the right chunk it is exactly the answer, but the relevant material may be split across two chunks and you only retrieved one.

    Important

    Chunk size is the first thing teams tune when RAG quality is poor, and there is no universal right answer. It depends on your documents and your questions.

    Overlap

    Chunks usually overlap by a small amount, often fifty to a hundred characters. If the answer sits exactly on a boundary, overlap means it still appears whole in at least one chunk. Cheap insurance against an annoying failure.

    For source code, the natural chunk is the function or the class, not a character count: structure-aware chunking arrived at from first principles.

    06

    Vector databases: what they actually do

    You now have a few hundred thousand chunks, each with a vector. For every question you need the nearest few, fast. That is a vector database, and that is all it is. An ordinary database answers "find rows where the column equals this". A vector database answers "find the vectors closest to this vector".

    Three operations

    Add

    Store chunks and their vectors. This is indexing.

    Query

    Give me the k nearest vectors to this one. This is retrieval.

    Delete

    Remove chunks when a document goes away.

    Why it stays fast

    Comparing your question against a million vectors one at a time would be slow. Vector databases build a structure that lets a query take roughly log-many hops instead of a million comparisons. You will never implement this: every vector database ships it. It is approximate: it trades a tiny amount of accuracy for an enormous amount of speed, and that trade is almost always worth it.

    07

    Making retrieval good: reranking and hybrid search

    Basic RAG works. Good RAG needs two more ideas, and these are where most of the quality difference between a demo and a product lives.

    Problem one: the top five are not the best five

    Embedding similarity is fast but shallow. It sometimes ranks a passage that shares surface wording above one that actually answers the question. The fix is two passes, and the analogy is hiring: you have a thousand applicants for one role, so you scan quickly to get a shortlist of twenty-five, then interview those properly.

    First passSecond pass
    CalledBi-encoderCross-encoder
    How it worksEncodes the question and each document separately, compares the vectorsFeeds the question and one document in together and scores the pair
    SpeedMilliseconds across millionsMuch slower, one pair at a time
    Can you precompute itYes, encode documents once and store foreverNo, every score is specific to that question
    Use it toNarrow a million down to fiftyPick the best three from those fifty
    Visualizer · RerankingScores illustrative

    Question: "Can I get my money back if the blender breaks?"

    Important

    The second pass is only affordable because the first pass did the narrowing. That two-stage shape shows up everywhere in search and recommendation, not just RAG.

    Problem two: sometimes you need the exact word

    Semantic search is bad at things with no meaning to speak of. Ask for "the price of SKU-4429" and the embedding sees "price" and "product" and shrugs at the code. Product codes, model numbers, legal section numbers, names and dates are all cases where you want the literal string. Keyword search, usually BM25, is excellent at exactly this and useless at synonyms. So you run both.

    BM25, the literalistVector, the philosopher
    Great atCodes, IDs, names, exact phrasesSynonyms, paraphrase, intent, other languages
    Blind toMeaning. "Marathon footwear" is not "running shoes"Exact strings. SKU-4429 is noise

    Hybrid search sends the question to both and merges the two ranked lists. The usual merge is Reciprocal Rank Fusion: each document scores based on where it placed in each list (here 1 / (60 + rank)), so anything both methods liked rises to the top, and anything one method loved strongly still gets a fair hearing.

    Visualizer · Hybrid searchRankings illustrative, fusion computed
    Important

    Real questions are messy: mostly natural language, sprinkled with a product code or a proper noun. Hybrid covers both halves automatically, which is why it is the production default rather than a clever extra.

      What you are actually looking at

      That numbered chip is the retrieved chunk. Everything in Sections 4 to 7 happened to produce it: your document was chunked, each chunk embedded, your question embedded, the nearest chunks retrieved, and those chunks put into the prompt.

      Important

      You are seeing box 2 of the five, the box you cannot normally see. In your Class 3 summariser there was nothing to show, because you retrieved the whole page. Here the system can point at the sentence.

      Important

      The refusal on a question your sources do not cover is the whole point of grounding. It is the behaviour you wrote by hand into your own app in Class 3 with "answer only from the page". Here it is built in.

      Is this doing the reranking and hybrid search from Section 7? Common doubt

      Google does not publish the details, and a production system from a major lab almost certainly does more than basic vector search. What you can observe is the part that matters for today: it retrieves specific passages and it can show you which ones.
